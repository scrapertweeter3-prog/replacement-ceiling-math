The durable version has a price ceiling, not a premium

Replacement cycles are the real price tag. A work bag carried 200 days a year shows it: a $60 bag that holds up for 2 years costs 15 cents per carry day, and a decade of that habit is 5 bags and $300. A $180 bag that survives 10 years costs 9 cents per carry, $180 for the same decade. Same job, $120 apart, and the expensive one is the cheap one.

The ceiling runs the other way. Five replacements in ten years mean the long-life version may cost up to five times the cheap one and still break even: $300 for the decade is the fair price of the 10-year bag. At $180 it lands 40 percent under its own ceiling, and the carry-day rate drops from 15 cents to 9 cents before stitching quality ever enters the argument.

Kitchen math runs on the same multiple. A $25 nonstick pan replaced every 2 years is 5 pans and $125 a decade; used 4 nights a week, 208 nights, that is 6 cents a night. A $90 pan still going in year 10 costs 4.3 cents a night over 2,080 nights. Same five-times ceiling, $35 kept per decade, and the only input is the service life, not a brand claim.

The ledger has one soft spot: the assumed life. If the $180 bag dies in year 6, its 1,200 carry days put the rate back at 15 cents, no better than the cheap habit, and the premium bought nothing. Break-even needs the cheap path to pass $180 first, which happens in year 4 when the third bag goes on the counter. That is why the failure record matters more than the sticker: the ceiling is real only when the service life is. Warranty language, spare parts, and how the thing actually fails decide whether the durable buy wins or merely costs more.
